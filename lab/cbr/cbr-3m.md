# Compute-Based Reality and the Three-Manifold
## Space as an Emergent Topology of Computation

### Abstract

Compute-Based Reality (CBR) proposes that computation is not merely something occurring within reality, but is fundamental to the constitution of reality itself. If this premise is taken seriously, space need not be assumed as a pre-existing container in which computation takes place. Spatial structure may instead emerge from relationships between computational states.

This paper explores the proposition that the three-dimensional space experienced by embodied observers can be understood as an emergent **three-manifold generated by an underlying computational relational structure**.

A 3-manifold is locally equivalent to ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space while potentially possessing complex global topology. This distinction between local simplicity and global complexity provides a useful mathematical framework for CBR. An observer may experience a continuous three-dimensional world even if the underlying computational structure is discrete, relational, non-local, or of no conventional spatial dimensionality at all.

Under this interpretation, distance becomes relational cost, geometry becomes an emergent representation of computational connectivity, curvature becomes variation in relational accessibility, and gravity may be understood as the resolution of computational gradients. Time, rather than necessarily being another dimension equivalent to space, can be interpreted as the process by which one relational configuration evolves into another.

The resulting hypothesis reverses the conventional relationship between physics and computation:

**the universe does not compute within three-dimensional space; three-dimensional space may be what computation looks like from within the universe.**

---

## 1. Introduction

Modern physics generally begins with some form of geometric arena.

Classical mechanics assumes three-dimensional space evolving through time. General relativity replaces this with four-dimensional spacetime. Quantum field theories typically define fields over an assumed spacetime background, even where the geometry itself may subsequently become dynamical.

Compute-Based Reality suggests a different starting point.

Rather than asking:

> What computation occurs within space?

CBR asks:

> What if space is itself an emergent consequence of computation?

This changes the direction of explanation.

Instead of beginning with:

**space → matter → dynamics → computation**

CBR considers:

**computation → relations → topology → geometry → matter and dynamics**

The three-manifold becomes particularly interesting within this framework because our experienced spatial world is locally three-dimensional.

The central proposition explored here is therefore:

> **Physical three-dimensional space may be the macroscopic manifestation of an underlying computational relational system whose stable local topology is that of a three-manifold.**

This does not imply that the underlying computational substrate itself must be three-dimensional.

Indeed, one of the most interesting possibilities is precisely the opposite.

Three-dimensionality may be an emergent property.

---

# 2. What Is a Three-Manifold?

A manifold is a mathematical space that can possess complicated global structure while appearing simple when examined locally.

A two-dimensional analogy is the surface of Earth.

Globally, Earth is approximately spherical. Locally, however, a sufficiently small region appears flat.

A two-dimensional observer confined to Earth's surface could therefore construct locally Cartesian coordinates without immediately knowing the global topology of the world.

A **3-manifold** generalises this concept.

A topological space \(M\) is a 3-manifold when every sufficiently small neighbourhood resembles ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space:

\[
\mathbb{R}^{3}.
\]

Thus an observer located anywhere within the manifold can locally identify three independent spatial directions.

Globally, however, the manifold need not be equivalent to ordinary infinite Euclidean space.

It may possess curvature, loops, handles, compact dimensions or other non-trivial topology.

This gives us an important distinction:

\[
\text{local dimensionality} \neq \text{global topology}.
\]

For CBR, this distinction is fundamental.

The world experienced by an observer may locally behave like three-dimensional space without requiring the underlying structure of reality to be globally equivalent to \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\).

---

# 3. Computation Before Space

Suppose that at the most fundamental level reality consists of distinguishable states and possible transitions between them.

These could be represented abstractly as a graph:

\[
G=(V,E),
\]

where \(V\) represents states or informational entities and \(E\) represents relationships or possible interactions.

Crucially, the graph does not initially require coordinates.

There is no intrinsic:

\[
x,y,z.
\]

There are only relationships.

One state may be connected to another. Some transitions may be easier than others. Some relationships may be persistent. Some structures may repeatedly reproduce themselves.

Spatial concepts could then emerge from this relational structure.

For example:

**near** could mean strongly or cheaply connected.

**far** could mean requiring many computational transitions.

**region** could mean a highly interconnected subset of the relational network.

**boundary** could represent a sharp change in connectivity.

**dimension** could describe the scaling behaviour of reachable relational neighbourhoods.

Space therefore becomes something derived from relationships rather than something required before relationships can exist.

---

# 4. From Computational Graph to Three-Manifold

Consider an enormous relational computational network.

Choose some state \(v\).

We can examine states reachable within computational radius \(r\):

\[
B(v,r).
\]

If the number of reachable states grows approximately as

\[
|B(v,r)| \propto r^3
\]

over appropriate scales, then the relational system exhibits an effective dimensionality close to three.

This does not prove that the system forms a smooth 3-manifold, but it demonstrates how dimensionality can arise from connectivity rather than coordinates.

Additional requirements would be necessary.

Neighbourhoods would need sufficient regularity.

Local connectivity would need to remain stable.

Topological defects would need either to disappear at macroscopic scales or manifest as physical phenomena.

At sufficiently large scales relative to the underlying computational resolution, a discrete relational network could then admit an effective continuous description:

\[
G
\longrightarrow
M^3.
\]

The smooth manifold would not be fundamental.

It would be an approximation.

This relationship is analogous to the relationship between molecules and fluids.

Water appears continuous even though its microscopic constituents are discrete.

Similarly:

> **space could appear continuous even if its underlying computational relationships are discrete.**

---

# 5. Why Three Dimensions?

This raises one of the most important questions for CBR.

Why should the emergent manifold have dimension three?

Conventional physics generally takes three spatial dimensions as an observed fact.

CBR potentially turns this into a research question.

Perhaps three dimensions represent a particularly stable computational regime.

There may be constraints involving:

- information propagation,
- network stability,
- persistent structures,
- causal separation,
- error correction,
- computational efficiency,
- topology,
- or the ability of complex structures to recursively interact.

The existence of stable embodied observers may itself impose dimensional constraints.

For example, three dimensions allow complex structures to pass around one another while still maintaining sufficiently constrained locality.

Too few dimensions can severely constrain possible interactions.

Too many dimensions may alter the stability of bound structures or the character of long-range interactions.

CBR therefore suggests a deeper question than simply:

**Why does the universe have three dimensions?**

It asks:

> **Under what computational conditions does three-dimensional locality become the stable attractor?**

If a sufficiently general family of computational systems repeatedly generated effective dimension three, this would constitute a significant result.

---

# 6. Distance as Computational Separation

Once space is treated as emergent, physical distance must also be derived.

Suppose two states \(A\) and \(B\) are connected through many possible computational paths.

Define a path:

\[
\gamma:A\rightarrow B.
\]

Each path has some computational cost \(C(\gamma)\).

An effective distance might then be represented as:

\[
d(A,B)
\sim
\min_{\gamma:A\rightarrow B}C(\gamma).
\]

This is conceptually important.

Distance is no longer an independently existing quantity.

Instead:

> **distance measures the difficulty of relational interaction.**

Two states may therefore appear spatially close because relatively little computation separates them.

States requiring much greater relational traversal appear far apart.

Ordinary geometry could emerge when these computational distances satisfy approximately metric relationships over large scales.

---

# 7. Geometry as Computational Accounting

This suggests a broader interpretation of geometry.

Geometry may be the macroscopic accounting system through which embedded observers describe computational relationships.

Length describes relational separation.

Area and volume describe scaling of relational neighbourhoods.

Geodesics describe minimum-cost paths.

Curvature describes systematic variation in those relationships.

The geometry is real from the perspective of an embodied observer.

But it need not be fundamental.

A useful analogy is temperature.

Temperature is unquestionably physically meaningful.

Yet individual molecules do not possess temperature in the same sense that a macroscopic gas does.

Temperature emerges statistically from microscopic dynamics.

Likewise:

\[
\text{microscopic computation}
\rightarrow
\text{relational statistics}
\rightarrow
\text{macroscopic geometry}.
\]

Space may be to computation what temperature is to molecular motion: an emergent but highly effective description.

---

# 8. Curvature as Computational Asymmetry

General relativity describes gravity geometrically.

Matter and energy affect spacetime curvature, while objects follow trajectories determined by that geometry.

CBR suggests a possible deeper interpretation.

Suppose the presence of persistent computational structures changes the cost or availability of relational transitions around them.

Then the effective metric inferred by an embedded observer changes.

Conceptually:

\[
\text{computational structure}
\rightarrow
\text{transition-cost variation}
\rightarrow
\text{effective metric}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature}.
\]

From inside the emergent manifold, the result would appear geometric.

From the underlying computational perspective, however, the same phenomenon could be described in terms of changes in relational accessibility.

Curvature would therefore represent something like a **computational impedance landscape**.

This is speculative, but importantly it provides a possible mechanism that could eventually be mathematically investigated.

---

# 9. Gravity as Computational Arbitrage

CBR has previously motivated the possibility of interpreting gravity as a form of computational arbitrage.

The three-manifold perspective sharpens this idea.

Suppose neighbouring regions of the relational network possess different computational costs.

A gradient exists:

\[
\nabla C \neq 0.
\]

Systems capable of transitioning toward configurations with lower unresolved computational cost would naturally evolve along this gradient.

To an observer inside the emergent manifold, such evolution might appear as acceleration.

Thus:

\[
\text{computational gradient}
\rightarrow
\text{relational reconfiguration}
\rightarrow
\text{geodesic behaviour}
\rightarrow
\text{experienced gravity}.
\]

In this interpretation, objects do not necessarily experience gravity because a separate force acts upon them.

Nor does geometry necessarily need to be regarded as the deepest layer.

Instead, both geometry and gravitational motion may emerge from the same underlying computational optimisation.

Gravity could therefore be understood as the macroscopic geometric appearance of the system continually resolving relational imbalance.

---

# 10. Time Need Not Be Another Spatial Dimension

The relationship between CBR and manifolds becomes particularly interesting when time is considered.

Relativity describes events using four-dimensional spacetime.

Mathematically this framework is extraordinarily successful.

But the effectiveness of the four-dimensional representation does not necessarily establish that time and space are ontologically identical.

CBR permits another interpretation.

Let:

\[
M_t
\]

represent the emergent spatial three-manifold corresponding to computational state \(t\).

Computation then generates a transition:

\[
M_t
\rightarrow
M_{t+1}.
\]

Reality becomes a sequence—or more generally a causally ordered evolution—of relational configurations.

Under this interpretation:

> **space is relational structure; time is relational change.**

Or more compactly:

> **Space is topology. Time is computation.**

The four-dimensional spacetime description could remain an excellent mathematical representation of the complete history of these transitions without requiring the entire history to be ontologically fundamental as a static block.

CBR therefore permits a distinction between the usefulness of spacetime geometry and the deeper mechanism responsible for generating successive spatial configurations.

---

# 11. The Present as a Computational Frontier

This model gives the present a particularly interesting role.

If reality is fundamentally computational, the present may represent the frontier at which relational states become resolved.

The past consists of computationally established relationships.

The future consists of possible reachable configurations.

Schematically:

\[
\text{Past}
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{Current computational state}}
\rightarrow
\{\text{reachable future states}\}.
\]

The future would therefore not necessarily be another location already present somewhere in a four-dimensional block.

It would instead represent a possibility space.

Time's apparent direction could then arise naturally because computation establishes new relationships while retaining constraints inherited from previous states.

The arrow of time may therefore be connected to the accumulation of computational constraint.

---

# 12. Matter as Persistent Topological Computation

If the manifold itself emerges from relational computation, matter must also be reconsidered.

Rather than treating matter as something placed inside space, CBR can interpret matter as **persistent patterns within the computation generating space**.

A particle could correspond to a stable local relational structure.

A field could represent distributed computational state.

An interaction could represent reconfiguration.

Mass could potentially correspond to the computational persistence or constraint associated with maintaining a structure.

The hierarchy therefore changes from:

\[
\text{space containing matter}
\]

to:

\[
\text{computation producing both space and matter}.
\]

Space and matter would then be different macroscopic manifestations of the same underlying process.

---

# 13. Embodiment and the Experience of Space

This becomes particularly relevant to consciousness and embodiment within CBR.

An observer does not perceive the universe from outside.

The observer is itself a persistent computational structure embedded within the relational network.

Consequently, the observer has access only to information capable of reaching it through local relationships.

This naturally produces perspective.

The observer experiences:

- here rather than everywhere,
- now rather than every computational state,
- neighbouring objects before distant objects,
- finite information propagation,
- and a persistent embodied boundary.

Three-dimensional space may therefore be intimately connected to the informational constraints of embodiment.

The observer experiences the manifold because the observer **is part of the computation from which the manifold emerges**.

There is no external viewpoint required.

---

# 14. Local Reality and Global Reality

Three-manifold mathematics also gives CBR a useful way of distinguishing local and global reality.

Locally, the universe can appear simple.

An observer sees approximately Euclidean space.

Globally, however, reality could possess topology impossible to infer directly from a small neighbourhood.

This has an important epistemological consequence.

An embedded observer cannot assume that locally observed structure describes the global computational system.

The principle can be written:

\[
\text{local coherence}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{global simplicity}.
\]

This may apply far beyond geometry.

Physical laws themselves could potentially represent locally stable regularities generated by a deeper global computational structure.

---

# 15. Singularities as Failures of the Manifold Approximation

General relativity predicts singularities under certain conditions.

At a singularity, quantities in the geometric description can become unbounded and the classical theory ceases to provide a complete description.

CBR suggests another interpretation.

Perhaps the singularity does not exist fundamentally.

Instead, the **manifold representation fails**.

This would be analogous to describing water using smooth fluid equations down to molecular scales.

Eventually the approximation ceases to apply.

Likewise:

\[
\text{smooth geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{extreme regime}
\rightarrow
\text{manifold approximation fails}
\rightarrow
\text{underlying computation dominates}.
\]

A black hole could therefore represent a regime in which the mapping between computational relations and smooth geometry becomes extreme.

The apparent mathematical singularity might mark the boundary of the geometric description rather than an actual infinite physical quantity.

---

# 16. Topological Change

Classical smooth spacetime places significant constraints on changes in topology.

A computational substrate need not necessarily share those constraints.

Connections within an underlying relational graph could potentially be created, removed or reorganised.

At macroscopic scales the resulting structure might continue to appear as a smooth manifold.

But during certain transitions, manifold-like behaviour might temporarily fail.

This opens interesting possibilities for understanding phenomena where conventional geometric intuition breaks down.

Topology itself could become dynamic.

Instead of:

\[
\text{fixed space} + \text{changing contents},
\]

CBR gives:

\[
\text{changing computation}
\rightarrow
\text{changing topology}
\rightarrow
\text{changing geometry}.
\]

Reality is therefore not merely moving through space.

Reality is continually producing space.

---

# 17. A Layered Model of Reality

The relationship can now be expressed as a hierarchy:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Computation}
\rightarrow
\text{Relations}
\rightarrow
\text{Topology}
\rightarrow
\text{3-Manifold}
\rightarrow
\text{Metric Geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{Physics}
\rightarrow
\text{Embodied Experience}
}
\]

Each layer provides an effective description of the layer below it.

At human scales, geometry is enormously useful.

At deeper scales, topology may be more fundamental.

Below topology lies relational structure.

And beneath relational structure lies computation itself.

CBR therefore does not deny geometry.

It attempts to explain why geometry exists.

---

# 18. From Three-Manifold to General Relativity

A serious CBR theory must eventually recover known physics.

It is not sufficient to produce something vaguely resembling three-dimensional space.

The emergent structure would need to reproduce, at appropriate limits:

- Lorentzian causal behaviour,
- relativistic invariance,
- gravitational dynamics,
- quantum phenomena,
- conservation laws,
- stable matter,
- and the extraordinary precision already achieved by existing physical theories.

This is an enormous requirement.

The three-manifold hypothesis should therefore be understood not as a replacement for established physics but as a proposed deeper explanatory layer.

The challenge is to construct a mapping:

\[
\text{computational rules}
\rightarrow
\text{effective geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{Einstein-like dynamics}.
\]

Without such a derivation, the idea remains philosophical and speculative.

With one, it becomes physics.

---

# 19. Falsifiability

CBR becomes scientifically interesting when it produces consequences that differ from theories that simply assume continuous spacetime.

Several potential research directions follow from the three-manifold interpretation.

### 19.1 Emergent dimensionality

Construct broad classes of relational computational systems and determine their stable effective dimensionality.

If dimension three emerges unusually frequently under conditions required for persistent complex structures, this would support the hypothesis.

### 19.2 Lorentz symmetry

Determine whether apparently discrete computational rules can generate Lorentz invariance at macroscopic scales without requiring extreme fine tuning.

### 19.3 Computational curvature

Define an explicit relationship between relational transition cost and an emergent metric tensor.

A successful theory should reproduce known gravitational predictions.

### 19.4 Breakdown scale

If smooth geometry is emergent, there should in principle be some regime where the approximation fails.

The theory should predict the scale or circumstances under which this occurs.

### 19.5 Topological defects

An imperfect emergence of manifold structure might generate stable defects.

If such defects have observable physical consequences, they could provide direct evidence for the underlying relational substrate.

### 19.6 Gravity as arbitrage

The computational-arbitrage hypothesis must ultimately reproduce quantitative gravitational behaviour rather than merely provide an analogy.

A particularly important target would be deriving something equivalent to geodesic motion from local computational optimisation.

# 20. The Central Research Question

The three-manifold perspective transforms CBR into a concrete mathematical programme.

Rather than assuming a universe with three spatial dimensions and then defining computation inside it, begin with a sufficiently minimal relational computational system.

Then ask:

> **What classes of computational rules naturally generate stable macroscopic relational structures locally equivalent to a three-manifold?**

If successful, the next question becomes:

> **Do the dynamics of those structures reproduce the geometry and physics of our universe?**

This provides a potential progression:

\[
\text{rules}
\rightarrow
\text{network}
\rightarrow
\text{dimension}
\rightarrow
\text{manifold}
\rightarrow
\text{metric}
\rightarrow
\text{curvature}
\rightarrow
\text{gravity}
\rightarrow
\text{matter}
\rightarrow
\text{observers}.
\]

Each transition can potentially become a separate mathematical hypothesis.

# 21. Conclusion

Three-manifolds provide a powerful conceptual bridge between Compute-Based Reality and physical space.

They allow CBR to distinguish between the underlying computational structure of reality and the spatial world experienced by observers.

The deepest inversion is simple:

> **Computation need not happen in space. Space may happen in computation.**

Three-dimensionality then becomes something to explain rather than something to assume.

Distance becomes relational accessibility.

Geometry becomes computational accounting.

Curvature becomes variation in computational relationships.

Matter becomes persistent computational structure.

Gravity may become the geometric manifestation of computational arbitrage.

And time becomes the process through which relational configurations change.

The universe described by CBR is therefore not fundamentally a collection of objects moving through a pre-existing arena.

It is an evolving relational computation whose stable internal structure is experienced by embodied observers as space, matter and physical law.

The 3-manifold is the interface between those descriptions.

At the deepest level, the proposal can be reduced to one statement:

> **The universe does not compute inside a three-dimensional manifold. The three-dimensional manifold is what the computation looks like from inside the universe.**

That proposition also establishes a path from CBR as a philosophical model toward CBR as a mathematical research programme.

The decisive challenge is no longer simply to argue that space could emerge from computation.

It is to demonstrate **why three-dimensional manifold structure should emerge, what computational rules produce it, and whether the resulting geometry necessarily gives rise to the physics we observe.**
